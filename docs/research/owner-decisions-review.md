## B3-1: Uncollected prescriptions and the deductible

**Status:** open. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** When a doctor prices a new prescription, should the price count the member's other prescriptions that are already priced but not yet picked up? And if one of those is never picked up, what happens to the other prices that counted it?

**Example.** A member's plan has a $500 deductible, nothing paid yet, and a $10 copay once the deductible is met. Monday: Script A, total cost $300. Wednesday: Script B, total cost $400. If uncollected scripts count: A is quoted $300. B is quoted $210 ($200 finishes the deductible, plus the $10 copay on the rest). She picks up both and pays $510, which is what she was told. If they do not count: B is quoted $400, so the office shows $700 for the two. At the counter B drops to $210, provided pickup re-prices (decision B3-2). She pays $510 after being told $700. If A is never picked up: B's $210 quote counted A, and B's true price without A is $400. Either the plan covers the $190, or B is re-priced to $400 and she is told before she collects it. Family version: the family out-of-pocket maximum is $6,000 and the family has paid $5,800. Dad's script is quoted $200, the last of the family maximum. The same week the child's script would be quoted $0 if Dad's pending script counts, or $150 if it does not. Without counting, and without re-pricing at pickup, the family is billed $150 over its maximum.

**Options.**
- **Count uncollected scripts for the member and the family. When a counted script lapses, re-price the other uncollected scripts that counted it.** Each new price adds the uncollected prices of the member and the family to what has been paid. A count lasts for the pickup window, about one to two weeks. When a counted script lapses, its count is released and the member's and family's still-uncollected scripts that counted it are re-priced, with a notice in the app. Scripts already picked up keep their price. Upside: The price in the room matches the counter for anyone on several drugs: $210 for B, $510 in all. This also matches how pharmacy claims count today. A claim counts toward the deductible when it is priced, and a return to stock backs it out. A member gains nothing by having an expensive script written to lower a later quote and then leaving it uncollected. Downside: This option is the most work. It needs a reserved total per member and per family, a release when a script lapses, and a re-price of the scripts that depended on it. It is also one more exception to the B3-2 rule that a quote is never exceeded. The plan still covers the $190 when B was picked up before A lapsed.
- **Count uncollected scripts, and every quote stands even if a counted script lapses.** This is the same counting. A quote never rises, so when A lapses B stays at $210 and the plan covers the $190. Upside: The promise is simpler: the price you were told is the most you pay. There is no re-pricing job. Downside: The plan covers the gap every time a counted script is abandoned. The member's deductible counter then reads $200 where it would read $400, and the plan gets that money back only if she spends more later in the year. It is open to gaming: have a $300 script written, collect the $400 one at $210, and leave the first on the shelf.
- **Count only what was picked up.** Each price reads only collected fills. B is quoted $400. Upside: This is the least bookkeeping. If B3-2's re-pricing at pickup is accepted, the amount charged at the counter is always right. Downside: The office price is too high for anyone on two or more drugs in the deductible phase or near the out-of-pocket maximum: $700 shown, $510 owed. A high price in the room makes people walk away, and that is the problem pricing at prescribing was meant to fix.
- **Keep pricing at dispense as the code does today, and show an estimate at prescribing.** The binding price stays inside the fill proof at dispense. The prescriber sees an unproven estimate. An estimator exists in src/access-control/benefit-check-service.ts, but nothing calls it. Upside: This adds no new money risk and the least work. Today's dispense already counts every dispensed fill, for the member and the family. Downside: It reverses the owner decision of 2026-10-16 that the price is proven before the script reaches the pharmacy. The estimate is unproven, and the existing estimator ignores the family caps.

**Earlier recommendation.** "(1) the pending delta: recommend the next adjudication reads the accumulator plus the member's pending settlements, so two scripts written an hour apart cannot both be priced against the pre-pickup total" (scripts/e2e-verify/PLAN-security-hardening.md, State of work 2026-10-18 Session 6, "Decisions the next items wait on", around line 2733). Section 7 (line 2216) says the same: "a pending delta must be visible to the next adjudication and reserved against."

**Assessment.** The direction still holds. It matches what the engine already does for the short wait on the phone's proof, but it needs four changes. What the code does now: (1) Nothing is priced at prescribing yet. createPrescription runs the formulary, prior authorization, step therapy, consent and network checks and marks the script ADJUDICATED. It returns only the plan copay and the list price. The patient's share is computed at dispense, inside the fill proof, from the accumulator at that moment. So this decision is about B3's flow, which is not built. (2) At dispense the engine updates its own copy of the accumulator immediately. The phone's proof of that update queues behind any earlier one, and the claim reaches the ledger when that proof lands. A second dispense a minute later already reads the new totals. So the engine already prices against what has been paid plus what is pending, for the minutes the phone needs to prove. Fable's rule stretches the same idea over the days between prescribing and pickup. (3) Today an uncollected script costs nothing to undo. A prepared (FILLED) script can be cancelled before dispense, and no money has moved. The reversal path accepts only a DISPENSED fill and subtracts its amounts from the current accumulator. Under B3 an uncollected script never reached the accumulator. Its reversal must release the reservation, write the reversal leaf so the pending settlement can never be finalized, and leave the accumulator alone. That path does not exist. (4) Family pooling (B8) feeds the fill proof the family's totals before the fill. Today those totals include every family member's dispensed fills. Under B3 they must also include each family member's open reservations. The earlier wording names only the member's own. (5) There are limits on what a reservation can rely on. The family totals are private inputs: the circuit commits to them but does not check them against the family members' credentials. The family deductible is passed in but never applied; only the family out-of-pocket and benefit caps act. A reservation total would be one more number the engine supplies, unless it is carried in the member's credential. (6) This review found a defect that is not yet in docs/AUDIT.md. dispensePrescription and reversePrescription rebuild the member's accumulator with fixed limits: a $500 deductible, an $8,000 out-of-pocket maximum, no benefit maximum and plan year 2026. They should use the plan limits bound at registration, and nothing compares the engine's new root with the proof's. After the first fill, a member on a $6,000 out-of-pocket plan is capped at $8,000, and a plan's $2,500 benefit maximum disappears. The harness hides this. run.ts registers a plan with a $7,800 maximum, then expects $8,000, with the comment 'limits are fixed in the adjudication service'. Every reservation design reads this record, so fix the defect first. (7) If B3-2 is accepted (re-price at pickup, charge the lower), the amount charged is right even with no reservation. This decision then sets how accurate the price in the room is, and who covers the gap when a counted script is never collected. An accurate price in the room is the reason for pricing at prescribing, so counting pending scripts still wins. The earlier recommendation says nothing about abandonment. With no rule, a member could have an expensive script written to lower a later quote and then never collect it. (8) D4's duplicate check keys on the dispense date and the pharmacy, so it would miss two open scripts for the same drug. The reservation check should refuse the second one at prescribing. The four changes: count the family's open scripts too; let each count last for the pickup window; when a script lapses, release its count and re-price the uncollected scripts that counted it, with a notice; fix the fixed-limits defect first.

**Recommended answer.** Yes: each new price counts the member's and the family's prescriptions that are priced but not yet picked up, and each count lasts for the pickup window. If one of those lapses, its count is released and any other uncollected prescription that counted it is re-priced, with a notice to the member; prescriptions already picked up keep their price.

**Unblocks.** B3 (price at prescribing, finalize at pickup), together with decisions B3-2 to B3-5. It also closes risk TEC-9 in docs/RISK-LOG.md and changes B3-2, because re-pricing after a lapsed script is an exception to the rule that a quote is never exceeded. **Effort.** About one session inside B3's estimated two. That covers a reserved total per member and per family that the next price reads, release on lapse or on reversal of an uncollected script, re-pricing of the open scripts that counted it, and a duplicate check across open scripts. Add about half a session to carry the reserved totals in the member's credential so the circuit checks them. Without that, they stay engine bookkeeping, as the family totals are today. The fixed-limits fix comes first and is small: a few hours, plus a test and correcting the harness's expected value.

## B3-2: Stale quotes at pickup

**Status:** open. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** The member is told a price at the doctor's office, and the pharmacy recalculates it at pickup. If the pickup price is higher, who pays the difference? Should the plan quietly cover small increases, or should any increase stop the pickup until the member agrees to the new price?

**Example.** Maria's plan has a $500 deductible and a $30 copay. (1) On Dec 28 the office quotes her atorvastatin at $30 because her deductible is met. She picks it up on Jan 3. The plan year has reset her deductible, and the drug costs $180, so the pickup price is $180. (2) In March she is still paying toward her deductible, so a $120 drug is quoted at $120. A price update before pickup raises it to $123.60. Under section 7 she pays $120 in March and the plan covers the $3.60. In January the plan would cover $150 unless that counts as 'materially higher'. Under the later summary, both pickups are refused and she needs a new prescription.

**Options.**
- **A. Always honor the quote.** Maria pays $30 in January and $120 in March. The plan pays an extra $150 and $3.60. Upside: The office price is a firm promise. There is never a surprise at the counter. Downside: The plan's cost has no limit. A member can get scripts written in late December to skip the new year's deductible. The plan can end up paying past its own benefit limits.
- **B. Honor the quote up to $10 over. Re-quote above that, and always after a plan-year change.** March: she pays $120 and the plan covers $3.60. January: the counter shows $180 and the reason (your deductible reset on Jan 1). She taps to accept or leaves the medication. She does not need a new prescription. Upside: The promise holds for small changes. The plan's extra cost is at most $10 per prescription. Nobody can use a year-end quote to skip a deductible. Big changes are explained before she pays. Downside: One more rule and one more plan setting to build and test. A large change can still surprise her at the counter.
- **C. Re-quote on any increase (the later summary).** Both pickups stop. She must accept $123.60 and $180 at the counter. As written ('refuse the finalize'), she goes back to the prescriber for a new script. Upside: The plan pays exactly what its rules say. The accounting stays simple and nobody funds a subsidy. Downside: A $3.60 change halts the pickup. That works against the reason the owner chose to price at the office: telling the patient the cost in the room so fewer people walk away at the counter.
- **D. No ceiling, the traditional way.** She pays $123.60 and $180 with no warning. Upside: This is how pharmacies price today. It needs no new logic in the circuit. Downside: It throws away the main benefit of pricing at prescribing. The counter surprise comes back.

**Earlier recommendation.** Two versions, both by Fable, and they disagree. Section 7 of scripts/e2e-verify/PLAN-security-hardening.md (lines 2217-2218, 2026-10-16): 'Charge the lower of the quoted price and the re-adjudicated price. The promise made in the office is never exceeded, the patient keeps any favourable drift, and the plan absorbs a difference only in the rare case where the price rose.' Also: 'If the re-adjudicated price is materially higher than the quote, the cause is benefit exhaustion ... The pharmacy declines, returns the stock and requests a fresh script.' The state-of-work list of 2026-10-18 (lines 2735-2736): 're-adjudicate at pickup with the quote as a ceiling, and refuse the finalize when the re-adjudicated price is higher, telling the member why'.

**Assessment.** Both versions agree on decreases: the member keeps any drop. That part should stay. They split on small increases. Section 7 has them absorbed by the plan. The later summary refuses them. Section 7 has the right shape, but its premise is wrong. It says a price rises between quote and pickup only when the annual benefit runs out. The code shows at least six other ways a price can go up. (1) Plan-year rollover. planYearRollover (src/adjudication/interactive-service.ts, about line 5048) zeroes every member's deductible, out-of-pocket and benefit totals. It happens every year and can be large ($30 to $180). (2) Formulary changes. updateFormularyEntry (about line 4649) can raise a copay or tier, add prior authorization, or remove a drug, and l2_publishPlanOverlay can publish plan changes at any time. Today such a change breaks a pending fill outright. The formulary entry saved at prescribing no longer matches the new formulary root, and step 11 of the fill circuit rejects it. (3) Drug price updates. NADAC loads once at boot today, but the planned formulary pipeline will push weekly updates. A member paying toward the deductible pays more when the price rises. (4) A released reservation. If decision 1 reserves pending scripts against the deductible, an abandoned earlier script frees that deductible, and the next script costs more. Example: script A ($400) is reserved and script B ($150) is quoted at $130. A goes uncollected, so B becomes $150. (5) Family limits. B8 put the family benefit cap inside the fill circuit (family rules 2 and 4), so a relative's fill can raise this member's price. (6) Partial fills. The circuit charges a copay on each dispense, so a script split into two fills costs $60 against a $30 quote. There is also a mismatch in today's code. The office estimate (l2_estimateCost, src/l2/l2-rpc-server.ts, about line 1640) uses NADAC plus a $3 fee and ignores family pooling. The fill uses AWP, which is NADAC times 1.2, plus a $2 fee, or whatever cost the pharmacy sends to /dispensing/dispense. For the same drug with nothing changed, the estimate is $33 and the fill is $38. B3 must fix one cost basis inside the quote. Benefit exhaustion may be the rarest case of all. The ACA generally bars annual dollar caps on essential health benefits, prescription drugs included, in employer plans, so the fixture's $50,000 cap may never appear in a real plan. Benefits counsel should confirm this. The later summary's refuse-on-any-increase rule closes the gaming risk. But it turns a $3.60 change into a new prescription. A new prescription is also more than the case needs. The member is at the counter at pickup, and the status rules already allow a reversed script to be adjudicated again (REVERSED to ADJUDICATED, src/prescription/prescription-data.ts line 266). So a re-quote that the member accepts with a tap works on the same prescription. Only a coverage change needs the prescriber: a drug removed, prior authorization newly required, or a member terminated. A plan-year change should always force a re-quote, because a claim belongs to the plan year of the pickup date. Otherwise a late-December quote could skip a $500 deductible. The threshold should be a flat dollar amount. A percentage gives no room on a $0 quote and too much room on a $400 deductible-phase quote. $10 covers ordinary price drift: a 5% move on a $120 fill is $6. It also keeps the plan's extra cost small and capped. The cap applies to the whole prescription, not to each dispense. None of this is built yet. PrescriptionData has no price field, and there is no pending commitment and no window for uncollected fills. So both versions cost about the same to build.

**Recommended answer.** Charge the lower of the quote and the pickup price. Within the same plan year, the plan covers up to $10 over the quote per prescription. A larger increase, or any pickup after the plan year resets, shows the member the new price and the reason at the counter, and they accept with a tap or leave the medication. Only a coverage change (drug removed, prior authorization newly required) sends them back to the prescriber.

**Unblocks.** B3 (adjudicate then finalize). It fixes what the pending commitment must carry: the quoted patient price, the plan year, the quote's expiry and the cost basis. It also sets the price rule in the finalize step. It helps settle decision 4 (mail order), because the finalize the member pre-authorizes needs the same cap: quote plus $10, otherwise hold the shipment and ask in the app. B3 also closes A-22. **Effort.** Small on top of B3, about half a session inside B3's two. B3 already has to add a quoted price to the pending commitment. The rule adds one comparison in the fill circuit (pay the lower of quote and new price, within the $10 cap), a plan-year check, a per-plan cap setting with a $10 default, and a re-quote screen at the counter that the member accepts. It also needs one pricing function shared by the estimate and the fill, plus a test for each way a price can rise (rollover, formulary change, price update, released reservation, family limit, partial fill). The amount the plan covers must be applied inside the circuit, so the accumulator, the ledger and the audit chain stay reconciled.

## B3-3: What a tap at pickup proves

**Status:** partly decided. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** When someone collects a filled prescription, what must they show before the system makes the charge final and records it as the member's pickup? And what happens when the member's phone is dead, lost or at home?

**Example.** Maria's drug costs $300. Her share is $40 and the plan pays $260. The fill waits at the pharmacy as pending. (1) She taps her phone. The phone signs a one-time code from the pharmacy terminal with her wallet key. The pickup finalizes as member confirmed: she pays $40, the plan owes $260, and $40 goes on her out-of-pocket total. (2) Her phone is dead. The pharmacist checks her driver's license, hands over the bag and records it. The same $40 and $260 finalize, marked pharmacy confirmed. Her app shows the pickup the next time it is on, with a button to dispute it. (3) Someone finds a card with her attestation on it and taps it. Under the recommendation the card alone cannot finalize as Maria. With today's code anyone who copies those bytes can present them for 24 hours. (4) A pharmacy marks 20 uncollected fills a month as pharmacy confirmed to keep $5,200 of plan money. Those members see pickups they never made and can dispute them, and the plan sees that the pharmacy's rate of pharmacy-confirmed pickups is far above the network's.

**Options.**
- **A. The earlier recommendation as written: Maria's phone, or a pickup flag she sets on her phone for a named person and a time window. No pickup on a card alone..** B3 builds a small delegated-pickup feature now, on the existing representative code (domain tag 3). Upside: Covers a caregiver in release one. Keeps a found or stolen card from finalizing a pickup. Downside: It builds part of the pickup work the owner parked. Naming a person puts a holder identity in the record, which the research design avoids. The research found the existing representative code unsafe to reuse as it stands. There is no path for Maria when her phone is dead, which breaks the owner's rule. And today's phone tap sends the same copyable 24-hour token as a card, so the phone path would not prove Maria either.
- **B. Recommended. Phone with a fresh challenge, plus a pharmacist-checked fallback that is labeled as such. A card alone never counts as the member. Delegated pickup waits for the parked ticket design..** Normal pickup: the phone signs a one-time challenge from the pharmacy, tied to the prescription, with the wallet's registered key, and the finalize is marked member confirmed. Dead or lost phone: the pharmacist checks the patient as pharmacies do today and the finalize is marked pharmacy confirmed. The member's app shows every pickup and offers a dispute. The plan counts pharmacy-confirmed pickups per pharmacy. The finalize record keeps a field for how the pickup was confirmed, with room for a ticket type later. Upside: The patient can always collect. A copied card, a screenshot or a typed wallet ID cannot finalize as the member. Each record says how the pickup was confirmed, which helps catch a pharmacy that bills for fills nobody collected. Nothing about relationships is recorded. It works offline, because the pharmacy checks both signatures locally. Downside: In release one a caregiver relies on the pharmacist's judgment, as today, and the system enforces nothing for them. The pharmacy-confirmed path is the one a dishonest pharmacy would use, so it needs the per-pharmacy count and the member's dispute button. It adds about half a session to B3.
- **C. Phone with a fresh challenge only. No fallback..** Every finalize carries the member's own signature. Upside: The strongest evidence and the simplest rule. Downside: A dead, lost or forgotten phone means Maria cannot collect her medication. That breaks the owner's rule, and counter staff will find ways around it.
- **D. Accept a tap of today's attestation, on a card or a phone, as the member..** No change to the counter. The current signed attestation finalizes the pickup. Upside: No new code. Downside: The attestation is a bearer token on a passive tag. It holds no secret and no holder key, so anyone who reads or copies it can present it for 24 hours, and the same token also unlocks Maria's claim history at the sequencer for that time. It expires after 24 hours, so a card written once stops working the next day and does not help a member without a phone either. The record would say member confirmed when nothing proved it.

**Earlier recommendation.** "(3) what a card-only tap proves: recommend a representative pickup flag the member sets on the device for a named person and a window, and no card-only pickup otherwise." PLAN-security-hardening.md, State of work 2026-10-18 (Session 6 closed), "Decisions the next items wait on", lines 2737-2738. Section 7 (line 2219) gives the reason: "A card-only pickup proves the card is present, not the member... The representative mechanism already in the code, domain tag 3, is where delegated pickup belongs, with its own authorization rather than by sharing a card."

**Assessment.** The first half holds and the code makes the case stronger. A card alone should never count as the member. nfc-attestation.ts says nothing on the tag is secret and the nonce is no authentication factor. The attestation is valid for 24 hours by default and carries no holder key. The research also found it works as a read credential for the member's claims (l2-rpc-server.ts:2907 and attestationCovers). One premise needs correcting: the phone presents the same attestation, so a phone tap as built proves no more than a card does. For the phone to prove the member, B3 must bind the holder. The sequencer already stores each wallet's signing key (signingPublicKeyHex, since A10b), so the attestation can carry that key and the phone can sign a fresh challenge from the pharmacy. The second half should come out of B3. The owner parked delegated pickup on 2026-10-18. The research replaces the named-person flag with prescription-bound tickets that record no holder or relationship. It also found the existing delegation and representative code unsafe to reuse: unsalted guessable leaves, 128 slots for the whole network, nothing persisted, operator-only creation, and the relationship code made public. What the earlier recommendation lacks is the owner's rule that the patient can always collect. It offers no path for a dead or lost phone. Today the dispense takes no member credential (interactive-service.ts:3038) and the pharmacy marks a prescription picked up on its own word (interactive-service.ts:3609). So a pharmacist-checked pickup is how the system already works. B3 should keep it as the fallback, label it and make it visible to the member. Release one has no members without phones, because enrollment runs on the phone. Children and others without phones belong to the parked work. One more gap: /nfc/scan still treats a typed wallet ID as a scan (pharmacy-routes.ts:1005). Reading the code, without running it, the sequencer's D1 gate should refuse the history read behind that path, because the route passes no attestation to the read (line 1028). The same omission means a valid tap gets no history from that route either. The fix is planned with D8 and has to land before the finalize uses this route. Decide now for B3: a card or a bare wallet ID never finalizes as the member; the phone finalizes with a fresh signed challenge; a labeled pharmacist-checked fallback exists; the finalize record says how the pickup was confirmed. These can wait for the parked pickup work: pickup tickets for helpers, custodial wallets for children, co-parents, a minor's confidential care, power-of-attorney checks, secure-element family cards, and the photo-ID rules for controlled substances. How long an attestation may be honored while the pharmacy is offline is section 8 question 6, which is still open.

**Recommended answer.** Yes to no card-only pickup, with three changes. The phone must sign a fresh challenge from the pharmacy. A pharmacist-checked pickup stays available, labeled and shown to the member, so the patient can always collect. The named-representative flag comes out of B3 and waits for the parked pickup-ticket work.

**Unblocks.** The finalize step of B3: what a finalize must carry, who may send it, and what the record says about how the pickup was confirmed. It also sets the target for the /nfc/scan fix planned with D8. **Effort.** About half a session inside B3. Add the wallet's signing key to the attestation, add a one-time challenge at the counter and a signature on the phone, add a field to the finalize saying how the pickup was confirmed, and build the pharmacist-checked path with a member notice and a per-pharmacy count. The /nfc/scan bare-ID fix is already planned with D8. Delegated pickup stays out. The research estimates 25 to 35 engineer-days for it when it resumes.

## B3-4: Mail order: when the claim becomes final

**Status:** open. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** A mail-order pharmacy ships to the member's home, so there is no counter where the member taps a card to finish the sale. When should a mail-order claim become final and billed, and what does the member approve in advance?

**Example.** Maria takes a preferred-brand drug that her plan sends as a 90-day supply by mail. The box costs $420. Her deductible is $500 and she has paid $300 of it. At the prescriber on October 18 the quote is $235, for example the $200 left on her deductible plus a $35 copay. In the app she approves: this script, this mail-order pharmacy, up to $235, shipped by November 1. On October 22 the pharmacy re-checks the price, gets $235, ships, and the claim becomes final: Maria pays $235 and the plan pays $185. If the re-check had come out lower she would pay the lower figure. If the box never arrives, she taps 'not received' by November 5. The claim reverses through the existing reversal proof, her $235 comes back and her deductible and out-of-pocket totals drop back. If instead her plan's annual benefit had run out and the new price were $420, the box would stay on the shelf and her app would ask her to approve $420 or cancel. If she does nothing by November 1, the approval expires and nothing is billed.

**Options.**
- **1. The member approves at prescribing. The pharmacy finalizes when it ships. The member has 14 days to report a missing package (recommended).** The claim is final and billed on the ship date. A missing or returned package is reported in the app or by the pharmacy and reverses the claim. Upside: Matches how mail-order pharmacies bill today. No extra step for the member when the price does not rise. Uses statuses and the reversal proof that already exist. Downside: The member is billed before the box arrives. A lost package needs the member to report it.
- **2. The member approves at prescribing. The claim stays pending until the member confirms receipt in the app, and finalizes on its own after 10 days with no report.** The claim is final on receipt, or at the timeout. Upside: Billing follows receipt, which is closer to the counter rule. Downside: Most members will not press the button, so in practice this is option 1 with a 10-day delay. The pharmacy is paid later, and the reserved amount sits on the member's accumulator longer, which affects the quotes for her other scripts.
- **3. Ask the member in the app at ship time, on every fill.** The pharmacy prepares the order, asks for approval at the real price, and ships after the member says yes. Upside: Consent is given at the real price. No ceiling logic is needed. Downside: An extra step on every fill. A member who does not answer leaves a prepared order waiting. Slower shipping.
- **4. The pharmacy finalizes on its own when it ships, which is what the code does today for every fill.** No member action at any point. Upside: No new work. Downside: Drops the B3 rule that the member authorizes each finalize. A pharmacy could bill a fill nobody ordered, and the member would learn of it afterwards.
- **5. Leave mail order out of release one.** B3 supports counter pickup only. The pending record keeps a mode field so mail order can be added later. Upside: A smaller B3 and a faster pilot. Downside: Many employer plans send 90-day maintenance drugs to mail order. A pilot employer with that benefit could not use it, and its members would be pushed to retail.

**Earlier recommendation.** "(4) mail order: recommend a finalize the member pre-authorizes at prescribing, bound to the script and a delivery window." Written by Fable in scripts/e2e-verify/PLAN-security-hardening.md, State of work 2026-10-18 (Session 6 closed), 'Decisions the next items wait on', around lines 2736-2740. The question itself is in section 7, line 2220: 'Mail order has no POS moment. It needs either a delivery-confirmation equivalent or a finalize the member pre-authorizes at prescribing. Decide before B3 ships, because it changes what the pending commitment has to carry.'

**Assessment.** The direction still holds. The code today adds detail it did not have.

Mail order is not modelled. The engine loads its pharmacies from src/data/pharmacies.json: 15 pharmacies, all type 01 'Community/Retail'. An older seed list, src/seed/pharmacies.ts, has one 'RxDirect Mail' pharmacy of type 04, and the engine does not load it. The provider registry stores a pharmacyType string, and no code acts on it. There is no ship status, ship-by date, delivery confirmation or not-received path.

B3 is not built either. Prescribing shows only the tier copay. The price is still computed at dispense, inside the fulfillment proof. The dispense route takes a prescription ID and needs no act from the member ('No credential needed, fills are reversible'). So mail order works today only because the counter needs no tap either. The gap opens when B3 makes the member's tap the finalize.

Three later changes help. First, B1 step two moved accumulator proving to the member's device, which looks for queued updates every 20 seconds. A member at home is online, so mail order does not need the offline attestation at all. The device can sign the approval with its wallet key, the same way it already signs the primary-care choice. Second, the existing status rules already fit mail order. Shipped maps to DISPENSED. A lost or returned box maps to DISPENSED then REVERSED, using the reversal proof already in the circuit. A held box the member declines maps to FILLED then CANCELLED. No circuit change is needed for statuses. Third, B8 put family pooling in the fill proof, so a family member's fill can move the price between the quote and the ship date. The price-ceiling rule from decision 2 covers that.

One gap affects retail too. Pending claims on the sequencer have no expiry. A claim whose wallet update the device never proves waits forever. Mail order makes the pending time longer, so B3 needs an expiry rule either way.

What should change in the recommendation. (a) Finalize at shipment, with a not-received window after it. 'A delivery window' did not say which moment makes the claim final. (b) The approval carries a price ceiling equal to the quote. A higher price needs a fresh yes in the app, and nothing ships until then. (c) Each fill gets its own approval. A refill is quoted and approved in the app when it is due. Automatic refills stay out of release one. (d) The address and tracking number stay with the pharmacy, which already holds the patient's details as a covered entity. At most a hash of the tracking number goes on the network. (e) The member can always cancel before shipment and always report a missing box. This matches the owner's rule that the patient can always collect their own medication.

What the pending record has to carry: the script commitment and fill number, the pharmacy's wallet ID, a mail-order flag, the price ceiling in cents, the reserved accumulator amount, the ship-by date, the length of the not-received window, and the device's signature. At shipment the pharmacy adds the ship date as the date of service and, optionally, the tracking hash.

**Recommended answer.** Yes, with changes. At prescribing the member approves in the app a mail-order fill for that script, that pharmacy, a price no higher than the quote and a ship-by date. The pharmacy finalizes when it ships, and the member has 14 days to report a missing box, which reverses the claim. If the price rose, nothing ships until the member says yes in the app.

**Unblocks.** The shape of B3's pending record, so B3 is built once. It also settles the expiry rule for pending claims, which counter pickup needs as well. **Effort.** About half a session to one session added to B3. The work: a mail-order flag and a wallet-signed approval at prescribing, a pharmacy ship route with the ceiling check, an expiry sweep for approvals and pending claims, a member not-received route into the existing reversal, and acceptance tests plus a harness step. If the pilot plan has no mail-order benefit, B3 can carry the fields now and the ship route can follow the pilot.

## B3-5: Who proves the accumulator update

**Status:** partly decided. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** When a fill changes a member's deductible and out-of-pocket totals, who makes the proof that the new totals follow the plan's rules: the plan's adjudication engine or the pharmacy? And does the member's phone get to check that proof before it signs off on the new totals?

**Example.** Maria's plan has a $250 deductible and a $10 copay. She fills a $300 prescription. The rules say she pays $260 ($250 deductible plus $10 copay), the plan pays $40, and her deductible is now met. Under the recommendation, her phone receives the fill proof and the numbers (deductible paid $0 to $250 of $250, out-of-pocket $0 to $260). It checks the proof, then signs the new totals. Today her phone gets only hashes and signs whatever it is given. The engine also rewrites her deductible limit to a fixed $500 after the fill, so her next $300 fill would cost her $260 again when the plan says $10. That is a $250 overcharge that nothing catches. A phone that checks the proof would refuse that update.

**Options.**
- **A. The plan's engine makes the fill proof. The network verifies it. The member's phone checks it before signing (recommended)..** Each pending wallet update carries the fill proof and the old and new totals. The phone verifies the proof, checks that the numbers and roots match, and only then proves its wallet update. The sequencer verifies the same proof at submission and attaches the claim only when the member's update reaches the proof's new root. Upside: A wrong charge or a wrong total is caught twice: once by the network and once by the member's own phone. Closes the money half of A-22. Fits pricing at prescribing, because the engine already holds the formulary and the plan rules. Downside: The fill proof needs one more output, the member's starting credential state. That changes the verification key, which costs nothing at genesis. The phone must carry the fill circuit's verification key and spend a few seconds verifying. The engine still runs in the operator's process until R3.
- **B. The plan's engine makes the fill proof. The network verifies it. The phone trusts the network..** Same as A, without the check on the phone. Upside: Less work on the phone. The network still refuses a wrong proof. Downside: The phone keeps signing totals it cannot read. The member has no independent way to see that the charge was right. The witness in the earlier recommendation is dropped.
- **C. The pharmacy makes the fill proof on its own system..** The pharmacy node runs the fill circuit with its own key and submits the proof. Upside: The operator no longer makes the proof that moves money. Matches the familiar habit of a pharmacy submitting a claim. Downside: Needs B4 pharmacy keys first. The pharmacy would have to receive the member's totals and the family's totals, so it would learn how much the member's family has spent. Three proofs of 25 to 45 seconds each at the counter. Conflicts with the owner's decision to price at prescribing, before any pharmacy is chosen.
- **D. Keep today's arrangement..** The engine makes the fill proof and throws it away. The phone signs whatever totals the engine hands it, after checking only that the hashes add up. Upside: No work. Downside: No one outside the engine can check a charge, so A-22 stays open. Errors pass silently. Today the engine resets every member's deductible limit to $500 and out-of-pocket maximum to $8,000 after the first fill, and no check notices.

**Earlier recommendation.** Recommend the plan service, with the member's device receiving a verifiable witness of it at pickup. (PLAN-security-hardening.md, state of work 2026-10-18, "Decisions the next items wait on", item 5, lines 2740-2741. The question itself is section 8, item 4, line 2262.)

**Assessment.** Half of this question is already answered by B1 and B2. The phone now proves every change to its own wallet, including after each fill. The engine computes the new credential, queues it, and commits the claim on the sequencer. The claim lands only when the phone's proof lands. So the question 'who proves the wallet side' is settled: the phone does.

The question's wording is also out of date. It talks about a 'plan-side accumulator record'. Section 7 (owner decision, 2026-10-16) and the B3 row keep the accumulator on the member's credential, and B1/B2 built it that way. What is left is narrower: who makes the proof that the price and the new totals follow the rules, whether that proof is kept, and whether the phone can check it.

The engine should keep making the fill proof. The pharmacy option no longer fits. The owner chose to price at prescribing, before a pharmacy is involved. B8 made the family's totals an input to the fill proof, so a pharmacy that proves would learn the family's spending. And pharmacies have no keys of their own until B4. Having the member's phone make the fill proof is not workable either: the circuit needs the pharmacy's identity inputs and the family's totals.

Where the engine runs: it is still inside the leviathan process (start-leviathan.ts lines 82-83 and 469-470). Step two of the engine move, its own process, has not started, and section 8 question 3 has no recorded answer. So 'the plan service' today means the operator's process. That is acceptable for this decision. A proof that is kept and verified can be checked no matter who made it. Since B2 the engine holds no member preimage, so it cannot sign as the member. It still holds the pharmacy and prescriber keys until B4, which is R3's remaining reason.

The part that should change is the witness. 'At pickup' made sense when the phone was not involved in fills. Now the phone signs every update, so the check belongs before it signs, every time. Today it cannot check anything. It receives only hashes (wallet-update.ts) and confirms they add up to the root it is asked to prove (member-routes.ts lines 120-130). The fill proof is thrown away (A-22).

B8 is incomplete on one point. The engine takes the amounts and the deductible credit from the proof, then rebuilds the accumulator with fixed limits: $500 deductible, $8,000 out-of-pocket maximum, no benefit cap (interactive-service.ts lines 3294-3295 and 3360-3368). It does the same on reversal (3791-3799) and at rollover (5064-5072, there with a $50,000 benefit cap). This undoes the plan limits that bindPlan sets at registration (1944-1953). Nothing compares the proof's new root with the root the phone is asked to prove. The single-member harness plan has a $7,800 out-of-pocket maximum. After the first fill the engine writes $8,000. The harness checks the limit only before any claim (run.ts line 436), and its reference model assumes the fixed limits (run.ts line 537). So the run passes.

The fill proof's output also lacks the member's starting credential root (accumulator-fulfillment-program.ts lines 42-68). It has only the new root. Without the starting root, a verifier cannot tie the proof to the member's wallet transition.

One consequence for B3: a claim lands only when the phone proves. A member whose phone stays off for a week holds up that claim. B3 should say whether the pharmacy is paid on the committed pending claim or only once the phone's proof lands.

**Recommended answer.** The plan's engine keeps making the fill proof. The proof is kept, the network verifies it, and the member's phone checks it and the new totals before signing its wallet update, every time. The pharmacy does not prove. Separately, fix the engine's fixed plan limits now, whatever the answer.

**Unblocks.** B3, which needs to know which proof moves the totals and who checks it at finalize. The money half of A-22; the prescriber-identity half stays with B4. A clean trigger for B7: the first verified fill can move a member from ELIGIBLE to ACTIVE. **Effort.** About 1.5 to 2 sessions, most of it A-22 work that B3 needs anyway. Add the starting credential root to the fill proof's output, then keep and verify the proof at the sequencer, reusing A1's verifier and pinned-key pattern: about one session. Link the claim to the member's update root and add the check on the phone: about half a session. The fixed-limits fix in fill, reversal and rollover, with a test that the engine's root equals the proof's root, is under an hour and can go into the next session on its own.

## S8-Q1: Member phone app platforms

**Status:** open. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** Which phones does the member app run on at launch: iPhone, Android, or both? And is it a real installed app that keeps the member's key in the phone's secure hardware?

**Example.** Maria enrolls through her employer. She takes a statin with a $30 monthly copay. Her phone holds her enrollment key and makes her proofs. On the development desktop a proof takes 25 to 45 seconds, and a registration can take up to 120. Suppose her mid-range Android phone needs 3 minutes. Then the D8 proving lease must allow 3 minutes, and one stalled phone can hold its partition for that long. At the counter she taps her phone or her card to collect the statin.

**Options.**
- **Installed app on both iPhone and Android, with one shared proving core.** Members on either kind of phone can enroll and collect. The phone's secure hardware (Secure Enclave or Android Keystore) wraps the key. Upside: Covers nearly every employee. Meets I3 and I4: no listening socket, and the key is held by secure hardware. Downside: The biggest build of all the options, and it is not in the current 13 to 18 session estimate. iPhones restrict apps that act as an NFC card, so iPhone users would tap the card or show a code at the counter.
- **Android first, iPhone later.** Only Android members can enroll at launch. Upside: Smaller first build. Android lets an app act as an NFC card. Downside: Leaves out about half of US employees at launch. An employer plan cannot do that to real members.
- **Web app in the phone's browser.** No install. The key and the prover live in the browser. Upside: Cheapest, with one codebase. Downside: Fails I3 and I4. There is no hardware keystore, and browser storage is where the audit found the member dashboard writing the real key in plain text (MEM-H4). Web NFC works only in Chrome on Android. Proving in a mobile browser is the least certain path.
- **Pilot on the current Node member device (the harness), on devices the program lends out.** Phase B already runs on it. Real members would carry a lent device instead of using their own phone. Upside: No app has to be built before a pilot. Downside: The key sits in an encrypted file on a computer, outside phone hardware, so I3 is only partly met. It does not test real members' phones.

**Earlier recommendation.** No direct recommendation was written. Plan section 8, question 1 asks: 'Which platforms, and does the first production target include a native app, or does phase B land against the harness first? Gates B1.' The target architecture in section 3 says the prover and keystore run inside the app process with no listening socket, the key lives in the platform keystore, and the Node member node survives only as a loopback development harness. RISK-LOG lists this as DEC-5, still open.

**Assessment.** Nobody has answered it. Half of the question is now moot: phase B did land on the harness. B1 and B2 run on the Node member device, which proves its own registration, credential and wallet updates. The repository has no mobile app. Three things have changed. First, D8 must size the lease to the proving time of the slowest device the network admits, so the phone choice now sets a security parameter. Second, o1js proving has never been measured on a phone. The only numbers are 25 to 45 seconds per proof on this desktop (A-33) and 20 to 120 seconds for registration (CODEMAP), so phone proving needs to be measured before any app work starts. Third, today's pilot estimate of 6 to 8 sessions does not include a phone app, and a pilot where real members use their own phones needs one. The dependent-pickup research adds a later need: one phone holding several wallets, for children who have no phone.

**Recommended answer.** Both iPhone and Android, as an installed app whose key is wrapped by the phone's secure hardware. First spend one session measuring proof time and memory on a mid-range Android phone and a recent iPhone. Then decide whether the pilot waits for the app or runs on lent devices with the harness.

**Unblocks.** Sizing the D8 lease window. A realistic pilot date. The I3 acceptance check on a real keystore. Later, several wallets on one phone for dependents. **Effort.** The measurement spike is about 1 session plus two test phones, a few hundred dollars. The app itself is many sessions and sits outside the current 13 to 18 estimate. Size it after the spike.

## S8-Q2: Who runs the network nodes

**Status:** partly decided. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** Who operates the network's server nodes at launch? Is it one company, the sister company, running all of them? Or do several separate organizations each run some?

**Example.** An employer with 2,000 members suspects a $40,000 billing error. If one operator holds every node key, that operator could sign any network record. The protection is that each employer keeps a signed copy of the network's state at every batch. If the operator ever shows two employers different states, the two signed copies prove it. With separate operators, a second organization would have to co-sign before any record is final.

**Options.**
- **One operator, the sister company, for release one. Keep the N-of-M quorum so outside operators can join later. Each node makes its own keys. Every employer gets the signed state certificates..** Phase E buys uptime and survival of machine loss. Honesty rests on the proofs that pharmacies and members check, plus the certificates employers hold. Upside: Fastest path, and it matches the owner's 2026-10-16 answer. Adding operators later needs no redesign. Downside: The operator could sign false records. Nobody would catch that until the chain proof (E6) and state certificates (E4) are switched on.
- **Separate operators from day one, for example the insurer, a large employer and an auditor.** Each record needs a majority of independent organizations. Upside: The claim that no single operator is trusted holds in production. Downside: Each operator needs a contract, onboarding and support. E2, E4 and the node admission flow must work first. It slows launch.
- **One operator on one machine for the pilot, with phase E later.** The pilot runs as the network does today. Upside: Cheapest pilot. Downside: Today the leader and both replicas run inside one process on one machine, and the replicas keep their state in memory. Losing that machine loses live proof state back to the last disk checkpoint.

**Earlier recommendation.** RISK-LOG, DEC-2 analysis (2026-10-16): 'Start single-operator for availability. Keep the quorum N-of-M as written so external operators can be added later without re-architecting: a large employer customer, the insurer, an independent auditor. Make detectability real now.' It also says to hand the signed state certificate to every customer, and, on seed backups, 'do not': generate each node's seed in its own keystore or HSM and never back up signing keys. Plan 7b leaves 'external quorum operators' out of release one, and R4 reads: 'Phase E as disaster recovery, not decentralisation.'

**Assessment.** The owner partly answered on 2026-10-16: about 10 nodes per million members, spread across the US, run by a sister company that provides seeds, installs and setup. The recommendation still fits, with three changes. One: 'provides seeds' conflicts with the earlier advice and with the owner's parked note that each node creates its own secrets (KSK-2024). The sister company should install and run the nodes, and each node should generate keys it can never export. Two: detection is only half built. A1 (proofs verified at submission) and D5 (money bound into the audit chain) are done, but the chain proof (E6) and state certificates (E4) are still off, so a single operator's misbehavior could not be detected today. Three: the engine boots inside every Leviathan process (start-leviathan.ts line 469), so it has to move out before several nodes run (see question 3). The 10-per-million sizing should wait for the R6 measurement of transitions per batch, because the cap of two per batch limits throughput.

**Recommended answer.** Yes to one operator, the sister company, for release one. Keep the quorum N-of-M so outside operators can join later. Each node makes its own keys and nothing backs them up. Every employer receives the signed state certificates once E4 and E6 are on.

**Unblocks.** The E1 and E4 design: node count, admission, and who approves a new node. The key custody answer in question 7. Sizing after R6. **Effort.** No new work now. Phase E is already counted at 3 to 5 sessions. Sending certificates to employers rides with E4 and E6.

## S8-Q3: Engine out of the sequencer

**Status:** partly decided. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** Should the pricing engine run as its own program, separate from the network node that keeps the ledger? Today both run in the same process.

**Example.** Maria's $120 asthma inhaler is prescribed and priced by the engine on node A. With three nodes and an engine inside each one, the engine on node B has no record of that prescription. If node A's machine fails before pickup, node B cannot finish the fill, and Maria has to get a new script. With one separate engine that reaches the ledger through authorized calls, any node can serve her pickup.

**Options.**
- **Move it into its own process as the first item of phase E.** The engine gets its own listener and credential. It reads and writes the ledger only through authorized calls, and it stays the one engine whichever node is leading. Upside: Removes the several-engines problem before it can happen. Ends the engine's direct write access to the ledger's trees. Does not delay the pilot. Downside: About 2 to 3 sessions, done after the pilot items.
- **Move it now, before B3 and the pilot.** Same change, done earlier. Upside: B3 gets built on the final shape. Downside: Delays the pilot by the same 2 to 3 sessions. A single-leader pilot does not need it.
- **Leave it inside the sequencer.** Nothing changes. Upside: No work. Downside: Every Leviathan boots its own engine, so phase E would run several engines with separate records. The engine keeps writing lifecycle state through adminMutate with no proof. The sequencer keeps working around the limit of one o1js context per process.

**Earlier recommendation.** Plan 7b, R3: 'Move the engine out of the sequencer. Keep it in phase B.' The reason given: 'The operator can impersonate any member, so I2 is false in substance whatever else lands.' The 'one real fork' paragraph calls it the long pole, about 6,800 lines, and recommends doing it 'because the product's claim is privacy from everyone including the operator.' RISK-LOG lists it as DEC-3.

**Assessment.** Part of it is done. On 2026-10-17 the owner directed the engine out of the visualizer, and B1 step one moved it to src/adjudication under the type checker. On 2026-10-18, B1 step two and B2 took the member preimage out of the engine. Devices now prove their own registration, credential and wallet updates, and A1 refuses any proof that fails verification. So the reason R3 gave, impersonating any member, no longer applies to members with a device. The process move itself has not happened. The engine is still built inside start-leviathan.ts (line 469). It reads the sequencer's trees directly and writes lifecycle state through adminMutate for registration, termination and provider seeding. It also still proves every fill with the fixture provider keys, which is B4's job. The reasons to move it now concern phase E: every Leviathan process starts its own engine, the engine's state file lives with one node, and its writes skip proofs. It should still move, and the right slot is now the start of phase E. It no longer gates phase B or the pilot. Because the identity half has landed, the remaining work is much smaller than the original 6,800-line estimate.

**Recommended answer.** Yes, move the engine into its own process as the first step of phase E. It does not need to block B3 or the pilot.

**Unblocks.** Phase E items E1, E4 and E5: several nodes, quorum and failover. Removing adminMutate from the engine. The target where the plan's service has no direct write access to the ledger. **Effort.** About 2 to 3 sessions, following the B1 step two spec. Replace the tree reads and adminMutate with authorized sequencer methods, move the checkpoint and events with the engine, give it its own listener and credential, and point the node servers at it.

## S8-Q6: Offline pickup tolerance

**Status:** open. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** If a pharmacy's internet is down, how old can a member's signed pass be and still work for pickup? And how quickly must a cancelled member's pass stop working?

**Example.** Sam leaves his job at 9 am on Monday and his coverage ends. His phone fetched a pass at 8 am. Today a pass lasts 24 hours by default, the device may ask for up to 7 days, and the network sets no ceiling. On Thursday a pharmacy with no connection accepts it and hands over a $500 brand drug, and the plan pays $460 it does not owe. With a 4-hour pass, the exposure ends at noon on Monday. With an online check, the pharmacy refuses at once.

**Options.**
- **Online only: every pickup needs a live check.** The pharmacy confirms status and revocation with the network every time. Upside: No revocation gap, and the simplest design. Downside: No pickup while the pharmacy is offline. That matches how most pharmacies work today.
- **Short offline window. The phone holds a 4-hour pass and renews it in the background. The network caps any pass at 24 hours. Online pharmacies always check status and revocation. Offline pharmacies may only finalize fills already priced at prescribing..** Pickup works through short outages, and a cancelled member's pass dies within hours. Upside: Bounded loss, and it works offline. Downside: A card cannot renew itself, so card-only pickup needs the pharmacy online. About one session of work.
- **Keep today's behavior: 24 hours by default, up to 7 days.** Nothing changes. Upside: No work, and a card stays usable for days. Downside: A terminated member's pass, or a copied card, works offline for days. Since D1 the same pass also lets a signed-in caller read the member's records until it expires.

**Earlier recommendation.** Plan section 7, 'Revocation still needs an online path': 'The time-to-live tiers already in the code are the right shape, with dispensing shortest at four hours. Production needs a revocation check when the pharmacy is online and a bounded staleness window when it is not.' RISK-LOG adds that the card must be revocable, using the nonce the attestation format already carries.

**Assessment.** The owner has not answered this anywhere, and RISK-LOG does not list it. The four-hour idea still holds, but the code is further from it than the recommendation assumed. The 4, 24 and 72-hour tiers exist only in AorService and in a comment on the delegation credential. AorService is never constructed, and F3 lists it as dead code. The pass members actually use comes from l2_requestAttestation. It lasts 24 hours by default, the member device may ask for up to 7 days, and the sequencer sets no cap. The pharmacy's /nfc/scan checks signature and expiry and never checks revocation. It also accepts a bare wallet ID, one of the two gaps planned with D8. The sequencer's revocation list is never filled and is held in memory only. Termination revokes only passes the engine issued itself, under its separate key. Two new facts raise the stakes. Since D1, a valid unexpired pass is also the pharmacy's credential for reading the member's records, so its lifetime is a privacy window too. And B5 already refuses dispensing for a terminated member at an online pharmacy, so the real gap is the offline counter. That gap only opens once B3 lets pickup finalize offline.

**Recommended answer.** Accept a short offline window. Phone passes last 4 hours and renew in the background, and the network refuses any pass longer than 24 hours. Online pharmacies always check status and revocation. Offline pharmacies finalize only fills already priced at prescribing. Card-only pickup needs the pharmacy online.

**Unblocks.** The B3 pickup flow: what a tap proves and for how long. Closing the bare wallet ID path at /nfc/scan. A persisted revocation list, alongside A-25 and F4. **Effort.** About 1 session, folded into D8 or B3. Cap expiry at the sequencer, add the online status and revocation check at the pharmacy, revoke outstanding passes on termination, and persist the list. A signed revocation list that offline pharmacies can cache adds about half a session.

## S8-Q7: Where signing keys live

**Status:** open. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes.

**The question.** Where do the network's most important signing keys live: in a cloud key service, in dedicated security hardware, or in files and settings on the operator's servers? And how often are they replaced?

**Example.** An offline pharmacy trusts the network's pass-signing key. Today that key is derived from the gossip secret, which is an environment variable. If the variable leaks, anyone can sign a pass for any member. A thief could collect Maria's already-priced $400 brand inhaler, where the plan's share is $360, and since D1 could also read her claim history. If the key lives in a key service, it never leaves the hardware, and a leak of the server's settings gives nothing to sign with.

**Options.**
- **A hardware-backed cloud key service for the few central keys (pass signing, insurer signing, genesis admin). Each node makes its own node and quorum keys in its own keystore and never exports them. Rotate with a key ID and a short overlap..** Central keys cannot be copied off a server. A lost node is replaced and re-admitted, and no key is restored. Upside: Low monthly cost. Fits the owner's note that each node creates its own secrets. Downside: The code needs a signing interface, and the chosen service must support Ed25519, which not every provider does. Signing then depends on the service being up.
- **Dedicated hardware security modules at each site.** Every key lives in hardware the operator owns. Upside: The strongest custody, and certifiable. Downside: Thousands to tens of thousands of dollars per unit, plus real operations work. More than release one needs.
- **Operator-held in files and environment variables, as today.** Nothing changes. Upside: No work. Downside: One leaked settings file forges member passes and employer attestations. Quorum keys default to a seed made from the public node ID. Fails F5.

**Earlier recommendation.** Plan F5: 'Key management: attestation and quorum keys in a KMS or HSM; documented rotation.' The section 3 target says the same. RISK-LOG, on seed backups: 'do not'. It says to make keys non-exportable and nodes disposable, generate each seed in the node's own keystore or HSM, treat a lost node as a replacement, back up state, and never back up signing keys.

**Assessment.** Not answered: RISK-LOG DEC-7 is open. A memory-index note records an owner idea parked on 2026-10-17: each Leviathan node creates its secrets with KSK-2024, to be explained before any design. That fits the per-node half of the recommendation. Phase A improved custody without putting any key in hardware. Every principal now has its own credential, the launcher makes node keys that live only in memory (A9), and the insurer signs with its own key pinned on the sequencer. The long-lived signing keys are still settings or derived from public values. The sequencer's pass key comes from ZKTRIDENT_GOSSIP_SECRET, or a fresh random key each boot. The insurer key comes from ZKTRIDENT_INSURER_SIGNING_SEED, or a fresh random key each boot. The quorum key defaults to a seed made from the node ID. The engine's own attestation key, used for provider badge sessions, still comes from a literal string in the source (interactive-service.ts line 647); retiring the badge service in B4 removes it. Rotation also needs a code change. The pharmacy fetches the pass key once and keeps it for the life of its process, so a new key needs a key ID and a period when both keys are accepted.

**Recommended answer.** Use a hardware-backed cloud key service for the central keys: pass signing, insurer signing and genesis admin. Each node makes its own keys and never exports or backs them up. Rotate the pass key once a year or on any suspicion, with a key ID and a short overlap so pharmacies with a cached key keep working.

**Unblocks.** F5. Quorum keys and node admission in E4. A pass key that survives restarts, which offline pickup in B3 depends on. **Effort.** About 1 to 2 sessions inside F5. Add a signer interface backed by the key service, add key IDs and overlap at the pharmacy, and end both the gossip-secret reuse and the literal seed. A cloud key service costs little to run.

## B4: Hardware keys for providers

**Status:** partly decided. **Earlier recommendation still holds:** yes, with changes.

**The question.** Should prescribers and pharmacies get hardware security keys, so their signing keys live on a device they carry? How much do we spend per provider? And do we also connect hospital ID badges?

**Example.** Dr. Lee writes 30 scripts a day. At the start of a session she taps her security key and types its PIN once, then prescribes. If she loses that key, her second key still works. A pilot with 20 prescribers and 5 pharmacy locations needs 50 keys, which costs $1,250 to $2,500 at $25 to $50 per key. A rollout to 500 prescribers needs 1,000 keys, or $25,000 to $50,000, plus replacements.

**Options.**
- **Two FIDO2 NFC keys per prescriber and per pharmacy location, with no hospital badge integration in release one.** Each provider's key is wrapped by a secret that never leaves the hardware key. The old passive-tag badge service is retired. Upside: No server can sign as a provider. The key itself enforces PIN lockout. If one key is lost, the other still opens the account. Downside: Hardware cost and a replacement process. Keys must be on the desk before B4 can be built and tested.
- **Pilot stopgap: each provider's key is made on their own computer and stored encrypted under a PIN, with FIDO2 before wider rollout.** Provider keys leave the engine and the demo tables now. Hardware comes later. Upside: No wait for hardware. A FIDO2 key can wrap the same key later, so the provider's wallet does not change. Downside: A stolen computer allows offline PIN guessing. It does not meet the EPCS rules needed later for controlled substances.
- **One key per provider.** Half the hardware. Upside: Half the cost. Downside: A lost key locks the provider out until re-credentialing and the wallet migration path (C3) both work.
- **Also integrate the hospital badge.** A badge tap opens the session. Upside: Ties our audit trail to the hospital's own access log. Downside: A separate integration for each hospital's badge system, and it adds no security factor.

**Earlier recommendation.** Plan B4 design note: 'Decision: a FIDO2 hardware key replaces the passive tag.' The provider's enrollment key is wrapped under the CTAP2 hmac-secret extension, NFC-capable keys are preferred, and the note says 'Register two keys per provider at credentialing'. Ordinary prescribing needs the key and PIN once per session. Open items it named: 'the per-provider hardware budget, since two keys each at roughly 25 to 50 dollars is 25 to 50 thousand for a five-hundred-prescriber system, plus a replacement workflow; and whether the employer badge is worth integrating at all, given that it is convenience rather than a factor once the authenticator is in place.' RISK-LOG DEC-6 is open.

**Assessment.** The plan records the direction, FIDO2 in place of the passive tag, as decided, and RISK-LOG calls it 'the FIDO2 decision'. The budget, the replacement process and the badge question are still open. Nothing has been built: provider-badge-service.ts and the dev badge PIN are still in use, and the repository has no WebAuthn or FIDO2 code. Four updates. First, B4 matters sooner than the pilot list suggested. The engine proves every fill with the fixture prescriber and pharmacy keys from the demo tables (interactive-service.ts, around lines 3049 to 3053), so no real provider can take part in a fill until provider keys leave the engine. B3, which moves fill proving to the pharmacy, needs the same change. Second, controlled substances are out of release one, so FIPS-certified keys are not required yet. Buy them only if the price gap is small. Third, the badge adds no security factor, so skip it in release one, which is where the earlier note leaned. Fourth, a pharmacy is a business, so one pair of keys per location fits better than a pair per pharmacist. The dependent-pickup research also names FIDO2 NFC keys as one option for family cards, so the same hardware and code could serve both.

**Recommended answer.** Yes: two FIDO2 NFC keys per prescriber and per pharmacy location, at $25 to $50 per key plus a replacement allowance, and no hospital badge integration in release one. Buy 4 test keys now so B4 can be built. If the hardware lags, use the PIN-encrypted stopgap for the pilot only.

**Unblocks.** B4 itself. Removing fixture provider keys from the engine, together with B3 and F1. A verifiable eScript once A-22 closes. Retiring the badge service and its key built from a literal seed. **Effort.** 1 to 2 sessions once keys are on the desk. Hardware: about $100 to $200 for development keys, $1,250 to $2,500 for a 25-provider pilot, and $25,000 to $50,000 at 500 prescribers.

## Contradictions between the recommendations

- Who makes the fill proof gets three different answers. B3-5 says the plan's engine keeps making it and the pharmacy does not prove. The B4 review says 'B3, which moves fill proving to the pharmacy'. The plan's own B3 row (PLAN-security-hardening.md line 2039) says 'the pharmacy adjudicates', and section 7's 'Latency improves' note says the fulfillment proof 'is generated at the pharmacy'. The owner should settle this first. B3-5's answer fits pricing at prescribing best. If it is accepted, correct the B3 row, section 7 and the B4 review.
- B3-5 and B4 cannot both work with today's fill circuit. The circuit takes the pharmacy's enrollment key hash as a private input and proves the pharmacy's identity (Z:\zpk_care\src\fulfillment\accumulator-fulfillment-program.ts lines 121-123 and 168-176). The engine fills that input from fixture preimages (Z:\zpk_care\src\adjudication\interactive-service.ts lines 3049-3053). After B4 the engine holds no provider key, so it cannot make this proof. The proof has to be split: the engine proves the price at prescribing, and the prescriber and the pharmacy each sign their own step with their own key. Neither B3-5's estimate of 1.5 to 2 sessions nor B3's estimate includes this split.
- The mail-order price ceiling differs between two reviews. B3-2 says mail order gets 'quote plus $10, otherwise hold the shipment'. B3-4 says 'a price no higher than the quote; if the price rose, nothing ships until the member says yes'. Pick one rule for the counter and for mail. I would use B3-2's rule in both places: the member never pays more than the quote, the plan covers up to $10, and above that the member is asked.
- Offline pickup cannot apply B3-2's rule. S8-Q6 lets an offline pharmacy finalize a pickup. B3-2 needs a new price at pickup, and B3-5 says only the engine calculates prices. An offline pharmacy cannot reach the engine, so all it can do is charge the quote. Example: script B is quoted $210 because it counted script A. A lapses this morning, so B's true price is $400. The offline pharmacy charges $210 and the plan covers $190, nineteen times the $10 cap. Either state that an offline pickup charges the quote and the plan covers any gap, and refuse offline pickups after a plan-year change or past the quote's lapse date (the pharmacy can check dates without a connection), or require pharmacies to be online in release one.
- Card-only pickup is ruled out in one place and assumed in another. B3-3 says a card alone never counts as the member. S8-Q6's recommended answer says 'card-only pickup needs the pharmacy online', which assumes card-only pickup exists. Reconcile them this way: a card tap only looks up the member and starts the pharmacist-checked path, which is labeled 'pharmacy confirmed'. Neither review says whether the pharmacist-checked path works offline. If it does, a terminated member with a dead phone can collect a $500 drug from an offline pharmacy with no status check at all.
- Offline pickup needs a pass-signing key that survives restarts, and only F5 delivers that. Today the pass key comes from the gossip secret, or from a new random key at every boot, and each pharmacy caches the key for as long as its process runs (S8-Q7). If the sequencer restarts with a new key, every cached key is wrong and offline checks fail. F5 is not in the pilot list. So either counter pickup in the pilot is online-only, or keeping the pass key across restarts moves into B3.
- A pilot on lent devices cannot test the pickup flow. S8-Q1 offers a pilot on lent devices running the Node harness. B3-3's normal pickup is a phone signing a challenge at the counter, and B3-4 needs the device to sign mail-order approvals. A member cannot carry a lent computer to the pharmacy, so every pilot pickup would go through the pharmacist-checked path. The per-pharmacy rate of pharmacy-confirmed pickups, which B3-3 relies on to catch abuse, would read 100% everywhere.
- iPhone users would always fall back to the pharmacist. The S8-Q1 review says iPhones restrict apps that act as an NFC card, so iPhone members would tap a card or show a code. Under B3-3 a card never counts as the member. Unless the phone can answer the pharmacy's challenge another way, every iPhone pickup is pharmacy confirmed. B3-3's challenge should also work by QR code or Bluetooth.
- The phone's check gives the member a veto that blocks everything after it. B3-5 has the phone refuse an update it cannot verify, and B3-3 gives the member a dispute button. Each wallet update is chained on the previous pending one (interactive-service.ts line 1741), and a claim lands on the ledger only when the phone's proof lands. So one refused or never-proved update blocks every later update. Example: Maria met her $500 deductible in 2026. On Jan 1 the engine queues the reset to $0. If her phone refuses or never proves it, the ledger still shows the deductible met, and every 2027 claim waits behind the reset and never lands. No review says what happens then (see the missing decision on payment timing).
- B3-1's re-pricing job overlaps with B3-2. B3-1 re-prices open scripts when a counted script lapses and sends a notice. B3-2 re-prices every script at pickup and applies the $10 rule. If the lapse re-price replaces the quote, it raises the member's ceiling without her agreement and overrides B3-2's promise. If it does not, it is redundant. Simpler: when a script lapses, send a heads-up only. At the counter, apply B3-2 against the original quote, with 'a script you did not collect was released' as the reason. B3-1's first two options then merge into one: count open scripts.
- B3-1's duplicate refusal can break the owner's rule. B3-1 says a second open script for the same drug should be refused at prescribing. There is no transfer path: TRANSFERRED is a final status and no code performs a transfer. When the first pharmacy is out of stock, writing a new script is the only way to move it. Refusing that script stops the patient from collecting their medication. Instead, the new script should replace the open one and release its count, and the prescriber should see that it did.
- B3-5 says a wrong charge is 'caught twice'. That does not hold for family pooling. The family's totals are private numbers the engine supplies. The circuit commits to them but never checks them (B3-1, point 5). The phone cannot check them either, because the other family members' credentials sit on their own phones. B3-1's reservations add more numbers of the same kind. The phone's check catches errors in the member's own credential, such as the fixed limits, and misses wrong family totals and wrong reservations. B3-1's idea of carrying reservations in the credential works only for the member's own scripts. A family reservation has no single credential to live in.

## Decisions nobody had listed

- **Pilot mode: shadow or live.** In the pilot, does zpk's price decide what members pay and what pharmacies receive? Or does zpk run beside the employer's current pharmacy benefit manager, which keeps paying claims while zpk prices them in parallel? Why: This sets how much of B3's money rules, mail order and durability must be finished before real members use the system. It also decides who sends the plan's share to the pharmacy. Nothing in the repository moves money. Suggested: Run the first one to two months in shadow mode. The current pharmacy benefit manager keeps paying, zpk prices every claim, and the plan reports each difference. Go live after that. Pilot pharmacies will have to record each pickup in both systems.
- **Children and other dependents without phones.** In release one, how does a covered child get a prescription filled? Why: Each person has their own wallet, enrollment runs on the phone, and custodial wallets are parked. A family plan would leave children's prescriptions, such as antibiotics, uncovered. Putting the child on the old system breaks family pooling, because the family deductible and out-of-pocket maximum would then be split across two systems. Suggested: For the pilot, enroll only employee-only coverage, or families in which every covered person is an adult with their own phone. Custodial wallets come with the parked pickup work.
- **Family deductible.** Do the pilot plans have a family deductible, and must release one apply it? Why: The fill circuit receives the family deductible but never applies it (accumulator-fulfillment-program.ts lines 397-454). Example: the individual deductible is $500 and the family deductible is $1,000. Dad and Mom each pay $500, which meets the family deductible. Their 22-year-old son then fills a $300 script. The plan should pay all but his copay. Instead the circuit charges him $300 toward his own deductible. Suggested: Yes. Add the family deductible rule to the fill circuit in B3. Changing the circuit costs nothing extra while production starts at genesis. If not, choose pilot plans that have no family deductible.
- **Drug price list and pharmacy fee.** What drug cost and dispensing fee does the plan pay pharmacies, and may a pharmacy send its own cost at dispense? Why: The estimate uses NADAC plus $3. The fill uses NADAC times 1.2 plus $2, or whatever cost the pharmacy sends to /dispensing/dispense. For the same drug that is $33 against $38. Under B3-2's $10 allowance, a pharmacy that sends its own cost could add up to $10 to every fill in the deductible phase, and the plan would cover it. Suggested: Use one price list set by the plan and published with the formulary, for example NADAC plus a flat fee from the pharmacy contract. The estimate, the quote and the fill all use it. The pharmacy cannot override the cost at dispense. A pharmacy that disagrees with the price declines the fill.
- **When the pharmacy is paid, and what happens when a phone never proves.** Is the pharmacy paid when the pickup is finalized, or only after the member's phone proves the wallet update? What happens when a phone is off for weeks, refuses an update, or is lost before the C3 recovery path exists? Why: Today a claim lands only when the phone's proof lands, and one stuck update blocks every later one, including the yearly reset. B3-5 raised this question but left it open. Suggested: Pay the pharmacy on the finalized pickup record. The phone signs every update that matches a verified proof. If a proof does not match, that is an engine fault for the operator to fix. If the member disagrees with a charge, she opens a dispute, which can end in a reversal and never blocks the queue. Flag any account whose phone has not proved for 30 days.
- **Quote life and pickup window.** How long does a priced script stay valid before the pharmacy prepares it? How long does a prepared fill wait for pickup before it lapses? Why: B3-1's reservations, B3-2's quote expiry, B3-4's ship-by date and the sequencer's pending claims all need these numbers. Pending claims have no expiry today. Suggested: A quote holds for 30 days from prescribing if the script has not been filled. A prepared fill waits 14 days, then goes back to stock and its reservation is released. The lapse date is written into the quote, so an offline pharmacy can check it.
- **Refills.** Who prices a refill, and when does the member see the price? Does B3-2's $10 cap apply per fill or per script? Why: A statin with 11 refills is priced once, in the prescriber's office. Refill 2 comes a month later with no prescriber involved. The code already counts fills per script (fillNumber, refillsAuthorized). Suggested: Price each refill when the member or the pharmacy requests it, and show the price in the app. Each refill gets its own reservation, ceiling and pickup tap. The $10 cap applies per fill. Automatic refills wait until after release one.
- **Changing pharmacy after prescribing.** If the chosen pharmacy is out of stock, or the member wants a different one, does the quote move with the script? Why: The code binds the pharmacy at prescribing: it checks the network (interactive-service.ts line 2492) and encrypts the eScript to that pharmacy (line 2755). TRANSFERRED is a final status, and no code performs a transfer. Suggested: Allow a transfer to another in-network pharmacy. The script is re-priced there, and B3-2's rule applies against the original quote.
- **Who pays for the quote guarantee.** When the plan covers a difference (B3-2's up to $10, a lapsed reservation, a gap during an outage), who pays: the employer's plan or the sister company? Is the guarantee written into the plan document and the employer contract? Why: Most employer plans are self-funded, so 'the plan pays' means the employer pays. Charging a member less than the plan's own cost-share formula needs wording in the plan document. Example: 500 members with 2 fills a month, where 10% of fills rise by up to $10, costs at most $1,000 a month. Suggested: Write the guarantee into the plan document and the employer contract. The plan funds it, capped at $10 per fill, and the plan reports the monthly total to each employer. Benefits counsel should confirm the wording.
- **Partial fills.** When a fill is dispensed in parts, does the member pay one copay or one per part? Why: The circuit charges a copay on every dispense. A $30-copay script split into two 15-day dispenses costs $60 today, against a $30 quote. Suggested: Charge one copay per fill, on the first dispense. In the deductible phase, cost is still split by the amount dispensed.
- **B7: what makes a member ACTIVE, and what ACTIVE changes.** Which event moves a member from ELIGIBLE to ACTIVE? Does the member ever move back, and should ACTIVE change anything? Why: Nothing in the code reads ACTIVE. The eligibility circuit accepts both states (eligibility-program.ts lines 248-249), and the engine's fixture update path hard-codes ELIGIBLE (interactive-service.ts line 5469). Under B3, 'first claim' could mean the first quote, the first pickup, or the first proof that lands. Suggested: The trigger is the first finalized pickup whose claim lands. The move is one way: a reversal does not move the member back. ACTIVE controls nothing in release one. Since nobody sees it, B7 could also come off the pilot list.
- **Pilot durability.** May the pilot run on one machine as it does today, or must it survive losing that machine? Why: Today the leader and both replicas run in one process, and the replicas keep their state in memory. A-25's restart paths relax root checks. Phase E is not in the pilot list. If the machine dies mid-afternoon, claims proved since the last checkpoint are lost, and members may have paid at the counter for fills the ledger no longer has. Suggested: The pilot needs E3 (a write-ahead log, flushed to disk before each change), encrypted state backups kept off the machine (never keys), and a restore that has been tested. The rest of phase E can follow.
- **Messaging in the pilot.** Is in-app messaging switched on in the pilot? Why: Messages are only base64-encoded today, so the operator can read them (D3 is open). Messages between prescriber and member carry health information. Suggested: Switch messaging off for the pilot unless D3 lands first.
- **What price notices may say about other family members.** What may the app say about another family member's activity when that activity changes your price? Why: Under B3-1 and B3-2, a relative's script can change your price. A notice saying 'your daughter's fill used the family maximum' reveals care that an adult dependent aged 18 to 26 may keep private. The pickup research also covers a minor's confidential care. Suggested: Notices say only 'your family's plan totals changed'. They never name the person or the drug.

## Challenges to the recommendations

- B3-1: the gaming argument is weak, and it does not separate options 1 and 2. In option 1 the plan also covers the $190 when B is picked up before A lapses, which is the usual order. Most of the cost is timing. After B, her deductible counter reads $200 paid, so she pays the remaining $300 on later fills. The plan loses money only if she spends less than the deductible that year. Pharmacies already work like option 2: a return to stock backs out that claim and leaves other paid claims alone. Keep 'yes, count open scripts', drop the separate re-pricing job, let B3-2's rule handle the counter, and track abandoned scripts per prescriber.
- B3-1: carrying reservations in the member's credential would add a phone proof at every prescription and every lapse. That is 25 to 45 seconds each on the desktop, probably more on a phone, and each proof competes for the proving lease that D8 hardens. In release one, keep reservations as pending claims on the sequencer. The member's phone can check its own open scripts against the quote. Family reservations stay engine bookkeeping, and the trust claims should say so.
- B3-2 rests on a wrong reading of the statuses. A prepared script that has not been collected is FILLED. FILLED can only go to DISPENSED or CANCELLED, and CANCELLED is final (Z:\zpk_care\src\prescription\prescription-data.ts lines 197-230). REVERSED to ADJUDICATED applies only after a dispense. - B3-2: the $10 allowance is safe only if the pharmacy cannot set the cost. Today /dispensing/dispense accepts ingredientCostCents and dispensingFeeCents from the pharmacy (pharmacy-routes.ts lines 524-560). Tie the cost to the quote's price list before the allowance goes live.
- B3-3: the pharmacist-checked fallback should require the pharmacy to be online, so the member's status is checked. The dispute button needs an owner. - B3-4: fine in direction. Use B3-2's ceiling rule. If the pilot plan has no mail-order benefit, carry the fields in B3 and build the ship route after the pilot. The pilot plan design (shadow or live, coverage tiers) should decide this before B3 starts.
- B3-5: the effort is understated. Splitting the fill proof for B4 is extra work. The phone also needs a rule for engine-made updates that come with no fill proof, such as the yearly reset, plan changes and termination. For example, it accepts a reset only on the plan's renewal date and to zero. Without such a rule, the phone's check either blocks the reset or approves anything.
- B3-5's option C says pricing happens 'before any pharmacy is chosen'. The code binds the pharmacy at prescribing (interactive-service.ts lines 2452-2492 and 2755), so the quote can include that pharmacy's fee and network status. The real gap is a pharmacy change after prescribing.

It also breaks B3-2's plan-year rule. Each fill rewrites the plan year to 20260101 (interactive-service.ts line 3360), which undoes the new year that planYearRollover sets. The reset itself still uses a fixed $500, $8,000 and $50,000 (lines 5064-5072).
- S8-Q6: change the answer to online-only pickup in release one. The member's phone may still be offline: it signs the pharmacy's challenge on the spot, and the online pharmacy checks status and revocation live. This keeps section 7's goal that the member needs no connection, removes the offline contradictions above, and saves about a session. Still cap passes at 24 hours now, because since D1 a pass also opens the member's records. Plan the offline window together with F5's stable key.
- S8-Q1: the measurement spike is right, but the owner can decide one thing now. A pilot with real members needs at least an Android build that can answer the pharmacy's challenge at the counter. Lent desktop devices can test enrollment and pricing, but they cannot test pickup. The spike does not block D8, because the lease length is already a setting (ZKTRIDENT_PROVING_LEASE_MS).

That is acceptable if the pharmacy contract requires the pharmacy's own logs to name the pharmacist. Buying four test keys now is a $100 to $200 decision with shipping time, so take it early.

The B4 review says no real provider can take part in a fill until provider keys leave the engine, and F1 removes the fixture keys, so B4 or its PIN stopgap belongs on the list.
